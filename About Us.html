About Us


J.T.K Birding Log is a birding project dedicated to exploring, documenting, and sharing the fascinating world of birds. This website aims to bring together bird observations, species information, photographs, and notes in one place, creating a simple and enjoyable resource for anyone interested in birds and nature.

Through careful observation and appreciation of wildlife, J.T.K Birding Log celebrates the beauty and diversity of birds. The goal is to create a growing collection of bird records while encouraging curiosity, learning, and a deeper connection with the natural world.
Our founder's favorite bird was the Andean Condor. The Andean Condor was a symbol of many things in South America and it easy to understand why our founder loved this bird so much. Read on to find out.

With a massive wingspan stretching up to 3.3 meters, the Andean Condor is the true king of the South American skies! These spectacular, razor-sharp avators ride powerful mountain thermal currents, soaring effortlessly over the jagged peaks of the Andes for hours without flapping their wings even once. Symbolizing pure freedom, power, and the rugged spirit of the wild, they are one of the largest flying birds on Earth—a breathtaking marvel of nature that commands total respect!

Soaring at altitudes up to 5,500 meters, these majestic giants are the ultimate high-altitude survivalists of the animal kingdom! Andean Condors possess an incredibly keen sense of sight that allows them to spot meals from miles away while effortlessly gliding through freezing mountain winds. As nature's essential cleanup crew, they play a vital role in keeping their rugged ecosystem healthy and balanced, proving that they aren't just breathtaking to watch—they are absolute ecological heroes!

Fun fact about the Andean Condor (Vultur gryphus):

The Andean Condor has one of the largest wingspans of any flying bird in the world, reaching around 3.3 metres (over 10 feet). Instead of constantly flapping those enormous wings, it uses rising air currents over mountains to soar for long distances while saving energy. It is basically the master of “work smarter, not harder.” The condor looks at a mountain updraft and thinks, “Why flap when the atmosphere is already offering free transportation?” A surprisingly efficient strategy from a bird that spends much of its life calmly gliding above the Andes.
